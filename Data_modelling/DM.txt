MODULE F1: WHAT IS A DATA WAREHOUSE?
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

1.1 EXPLAIN LIKE I'M 5: THE TOY BOX VS THE GIANT TOY LIBRARY
Imagine your bedroom floor has a small toy box. Whenever you want to play, you grab 
a car, dump out some blocks, and throw them back in when you're done. That toy box 
needs to be super fast to open and close so your room doesn't stay messy. That is 
your transactional database (OLTP) - built for quick, everyday playing.

Now imagine the mayor of your entire city wants to know: "How many red fire trucks 
did all children in the city play with every Saturday morning over the last five 
years?" If someone tried digging through thousands of messy toy boxes in every house 
at the same time, children wouldn't be able to play, toys would get lost, and everyone 
would get angry!

Instead, the city builds a Giant Toy Library (The Data Warehouse). Every night, a 
delivery truck makes a copy of the day's toy records and places them neatly into 
huge, organized shelves labeled by Year, Month, Toy Type, and Neighborhood. 
Children keep playing at home uninterrupted, while researchers and city planners sit 
comfortably in the quiet library reading five years of history in seconds.


1.2 WHY DATA WAREHOUSES EXIST & THE REPORTING PAIN THEY SOLVE
In any growing company, multiple operational software systems run the day-to-day 
business:
1. An e-commerce web app running PostgreSQL.
2. A payment processing system like Stripe.
3. A CRM like Salesforce for tracking customer leads.
4. An ERP like SAP for inventory and supply chain tracking.

If a Business Analyst runs a massive report like:
"Calculate our company-wide net profit margin by product category across all stores 
over the last 4 years grouped by fiscal quarter"
directly against the live production app database, catastrophic problems occur:
- Lock Contention: The report scans millions of rows, acquiring read locks on 
  tables. Real customers attempting to check out experience database timeouts.
- Siloed Data: Stripe knows about payments, Salesforce knows about customers, 
  and Postgres knows about orders. You cannot write a single SQL query joining 
  three completely separate databases hosted by different cloud vendors.
- Lack of History: Production databases frequently overwrite status columns 
  (e.g., changing order_status from 'PENDING' to 'SHIPPED'). Past states are erased.

A Data Warehouse solves this by extracting data from all operational systems, 
transforming it into a uniform structure, and loading it into a centralized, 
read-optimized analytical repository.


1.3 CORE CHARACTERISTICS OF A DATA WAREHOUSE (INMON'S DEFINITION)
William H. Inmon, known as the "Father of Data Warehousing", defined a Data 
Warehouse along four immutable characteristics:

1. Integrated:
   - Data comes from disparate systems with conflicting formats.
   - Example: System A encodes gender as 'M'/'F', System B as 1/0, and System C 
     as 'Male'/'Female'. In the warehouse, it is cleansed and harmonized into one 
     standard format across all tables.

2. Subject-Oriented:
   - Operational systems are organized around processes (e.g., Billing App, Order 
     Form, Shipping Portal).
   - Data warehouses are organized around core business entities (e.g., Customer, 
     Product, Sales, Inventory).

3. Time-Variant:
   - Operational systems show current state ("What is customer Priya's address right 
     now?").
   - Warehouses preserve historical snapshots ("Where did Priya live when she bought 
     that television three years ago?"). Every record is tied to time.

4. Non-Volatile:
   - Operational data is continuously inserted, updated, and deleted.
   - Warehouse data is loaded in bulk (batch or streaming append) and is never 
     updated in place or deleted during regular operations. Once written, history is 
     immutable.


1.4 INMON VS KIMBALL: THE TWO FOUNDING PHILOSOPHIES
In data engineering history, two legendary architectures emerged:

A. Bill Inmon (Top-Down "Corporate Information Factory" - CIF):
   - Approach: Build an enterprise-wide, fully normalized (3NF) relational warehouse 
     first, modeling the entire enterprise data model.
   - Then, derive departmental "Data Marts" from this single central hub.
   - Pros: Pristine data integrity, zero redundancy.
   - Cons: Massive upfront time (often took 2–3 years before a single dashboard was 
     delivered), exorbitant cost, very difficult for non-technical analysts to query.

B. Ralph Kimball (Bottom-Up "Dimensional Bus Architecture"):
   - Approach: Build subject-focused Dimensional Star Schemas (Data Marts) directly 
     from business processes (e.g., Retail Sales first, then Inventory, then Returns).
   - Integration across marts is achieved not via 3NF tables, but via shared 
     "Conformed Dimensions" (like a shared Customer and Date dimension) governed by 
     an enterprise Bus Matrix.
   - Pros: Delivers value in weeks, simple for BI tools to query, intuitive for 
     humans to understand.
   - Why Kimball Won: Today's modern data stacks overwhelmingly follow Kimball's 
     dimensional vocabulary (Facts, Dimensions, Grain, Bus Matrix).


1.5 MODERN CLOUD DATA WAREHOUSES (2026 ECOSYSTEM)
Modern cloud data warehouses have evolved beyond classic on-premise appliances:
- Snowflake: Cloud-native, completely separates storage (S3/GCS/Azure blob) from 
  compute (Virtual Warehouses). Elastic scaling in seconds.
- Google BigQuery: Serverless, SQL-native, columnar storage (Capacitor), charges 
  by query scan bytes or flat-rate slots.
- Amazon Redshift: Mature AWS-native MPP (Massively Parallel Processing) warehouse, 
  utilizing Redshift Serverless and AQUA acceleration.
- Databricks SQL: High-performance SQL engine built directly on open lakehouse 
  formats (Delta Lake).

1.7 EXAM & INTERVIEW MASTER TIP
Interview Classic Question: "What is a Data Warehouse, and how does it differ from 
an operational database?"
Winning Answer:
"An operational database (OLTP) is optimized for high-frequency, low-latency single-row 
transactions (reads and writes) that keep an application running. A Data Warehouse 
(OLAP) is an integrated, subject-oriented, time-variant, and non-volatile store 
optimized for complex analytical scans and historical aggregations across millions 
of rows without affecting production performance."

MODULE F2: OLTP VS OLAP - TWO DIFFERENT WORLDS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

2.1 EXPLAIN LIKE I'M 5: DRIVE-THROUGH VS WEDDING FEAST
- OLTP is like a McDonald's drive-through window:
  Thousands of cars arrive every day. Each car orders 2 burgers and 1 drink. The 
  order takes 10 seconds. The cashier only cares about one car at a time.
- OLAP is like catering a 1,000-person wedding reception:
  You don't serve one French fry every 10 seconds. You spend hours preparing giant 
  vats of food, analyzing exactly how many total kilograms of rice and potatoes 
  you need, and serving everyone all at once.

If you asked the McDonald's drive-through cashier to cook a banquet dinner for 500 
people right now in the drive-through lane, the entire line of cars would honk, 
freeze, and crash. You must never run OLAP queries on an OLTP database!


2.2 THE SIX FUNDAMENTAL AXES OF DIFFERENCE

+--------------------+-----------------------------+-----------------------------+
| Axis               | OLTP (Online Transaction)   | OLAP (Online Analytical)    |
+--------------------+-----------------------------+-----------------------------+
| 1. Query Size      | 1 to 50 rows per query      | Millions to billions of rows|
| 2. Latency Needs   | Sub-second (5ms - 50ms)     | Seconds to minutes          |
| 3. Write Pattern   | Constant small row writes   | Periodic bulk/batch loads   |
| 4. Schema Style    | Normalized (3NF)            | Denormalized (Star/Snowflake|
| 5. Storage Layout  | Row-oriented (Postgres/MySQL| Column-oriented (Snowflake/ |
|                    |                             | Parquet/BigQuery)           |
| 6. Concurrent Users| Thousands to millions       | Dozens to hundreds          |
+--------------------+-----------------------------+-----------------------------+


2.3 ROW-ORIENTED STORAGE VS COLUMN-ORIENTED STORAGE PHYSICS
Why are row-oriented databases terrible for analytics, and why are columnar stores 
unbeatable?

A. Row-Oriented Storage (PostgreSQL, MySQL, Oracle):
   Data on disk is stored row after row:
   [Row 1: ID, Date, Customer, Product, Price, Address, Description]
   [Row 2: ID, Date, Customer, Product, Price, Address, Description]
   
   If your query is:
   `SELECT SUM(Price) FROM fact_sales;`
   A row-store must read EVERY BYTE of Row 1 (including Address, Description, etc.) 
   into RAM just to grab the Price column, then read all of Row 2, and so on. Disk I/O 
   explodes, thrashing memory cache.

B. Column-Oriented Storage (Snowflake, BigQuery, Parquet):
   Data is stored column after column:
   [ID Column: 1, 2, 3, ...]
   [Price Column: 50.00, 120.00, 35.00, ...]
   [Address Column: 'Street A', 'Street B', ...]
   
   When `SELECT SUM(Price)` runs:
   1. The engine reads ONLY the Price column blocks from disk. The Address and 
      Description blocks are never touched! Disk I/O drops by 80%–95%.
   2. Columnar Compression: Because all values in a column share the exact same 
      data type (e.g., thousands of integers or repeated category names), algorithms 
      like Run-Length Encoding (RLE) and Dictionary Compression compress data by 
      5x to 10x.
   3. Vectorization: Modern CPUs can process arrays of column numbers in single-cycle 
      SIMD (Single Instruction, Multiple Data) operations.


2.4 VISUAL WORK DIAGRAM: ROW VS COLUMNAR BLOCK STORAGE ON DISK

  ROW-ORIENTED STORAGE (OLTP - Postgres/MySQL)
  -------------------------------------------
  Block 1: [Row 1: ID=1, Date='2026-10-01', SKU='A', Price=10.00, PII='John Doe', Addr='123 Elm St']
  Block 2: [Row 2: ID=2, Date='2026-10-01', SKU='B', Price=25.00, PII='Jane Doe', Addr='456 Oak St']
  --> Querying `SUM(Price)` must scan 100% of all data blocks including unwanted strings and PII!

  COLUMN-ORIENTED STORAGE (OLAP - Snowflake/Parquet)
  --------------------------------------------------
  File Block 1 (IDs):    [ 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, ... ]
  File Block 2 (Dates):  [ '2026-10-01', '2026-10-01', ... ]
  File Block 3 (Prices): [ 10.00, 25.00, 15.50, ... ]  <-- ONLY this block is read for `SUM(Price)`!
  File Block 4 (PII):    [ 'John Doe', 'Jane Doe', ... ] (SKIPPED ENTIRELY)
  --> Disk I/O reduced by 90%+; identical data types compress 10x with RLE and dictionary encoding.


2.5 CODE DEMONSTRATION: OLTP VS OLAP QUERIES

-- OLTP Point Query (Targeted, single-row retrieval):
SELECT p.product_name, ci.quantity, ci.unit_price
FROM cart_item ci
JOIN product p ON p.product_id = ci.product_id
WHERE ci.cart_id = 98421;

-- OLAP Aggregate Query (Scans millions of rows over time):
SELECT 
    p.category_name,
    d.year,
    SUM(f.line_revenue) AS category_revenue,
    COUNT(DISTINCT f.customer_key) AS unique_buyers
FROM olap.fact_sales f
JOIN olap.dim_product p ON f.product_key = p.product_key
JOIN olap.dim_date d ON f.order_date_key = d.date_key
WHERE d.year = 2026
GROUP BY p.category_name, d.year
ORDER BY category_revenue DESC;


2.6 HYBRID TRANSACTIONAL/ANALYTICAL PROCESSING (HTAP)
Modern database engines (e.g., Google AlloyDB, TiDB, SingleStore, Snowflake Unistore) 
attempt to bridge this divide by storing data in both row format (in memory for fast 
writes) and columnar format (asynchronously converted for analytics). However, for 
enterprise-scale analytics, dedicated warehouses remain the golden standard.

MODULE F3: WHY DENORMALIZE FOR ANALYTICS?
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

3.1 NORMALIZATION IN 3 MINUTES (1NF, 2NF, 3NF RECAP)
Normalization organizes data to eliminate duplicate information and prevent update 
anomalies in write-heavy application databases.

1. First Normal Form (1NF) - Atomic Values & No Repeating Groups:
   - Bad: Storing comma-separated strings like `products = 'Laptop,Mouse,Headphones'`.
   - Good: Every cell contains exactly one scalar value; each row is unique.

2. Second Normal Form (2NF) - 1NF + Full Functional Dependency:
   - Applies to tables with composite primary keys (e.g., order_id + product_id).
   - Rule: Every non-key column must depend on the WHOLE primary key, not just half 
     of it. If product_name depends only on product_id, it must move to its own 
     Product table.

3. Third Normal Form (3NF) - 2NF + No Transitive Dependencies:
   - Rule: Non-key columns must depend SOLELY on the primary key, and NOT on another 
     non-key column.
   - Bad: Table `orders` has columns `customer_id`, `customer_city`, `customer_state`.
     `customer_state` depends on `customer_city`, which depends on `customer_id`.
   - Good: Split into `orders` (referencing `customer_id`) and `customers` 
     (containing `city` and `state`).


3.2 WHY 3NF CHOKES ANALYTICAL QUERIES
In a normalized 3NF database, a simple e-commerce order is fractured across 8 tables:
`orders` -> `order_items` -> `products` -> `subcategories` -> `categories` -> 
`departments` -> `customers` -> `cities` -> `states` -> `countries`.

When a business analyst writes a query to calculate quarterly revenue by department:
1. The database optimizer must construct a complex 8-way hash join graph.
2. Huge hash tables are built in RAM for each intermediate join.
3. If table statistics are slightly outdated, the query optimizer picks the wrong 
   join order, spilling gigabytes of data to disk and causing query execution to 
   grind to a halt.


3.3 DENORMALIZATION MECHANICS & TRADE-OFFS
Denormalization is the deliberate re-introduction of redundancy for read speed:
- In dimensional modeling, we collapse the `products`, `subcategories`, 
  `categories`, and `departments` tables into a SINGLE wide `dim_product` table!
- Instead of joining 4 tables, the query joins only ONE table (`fact_sales` to 
  `dim_product`).

Trade-offs of Denormalization:
- What we gain:
  1. Fewer Joins: 8-way join becomes a 2-way join.
  2. Query Simplicity: Business analysts don't need to know 20 tables to write SQL.
  3. Lightning Speed: Columnar engines compress repeated strings effortlessly.
- What we pay:
  1. Redundant Storage: The category name 'Electronics' is duplicated across 10,000 
     product rows. (Storage is cheap in 2026, analyst time is expensive!)
  2. Update Complexity: Updating a category name requires updating multiple rows. 
     (In warehouses, this is handled gracefully by batch ETL and SCD techniques).


3.4 STAR SCHEMA VS ONE BIG TABLE (OBT)
Two primary analytical denormalization patterns exist:
1. Star Schema (Kimball): Fact table in the center surrounded by a handful of wide 
   dimension tables. Preserves modularity, allows conformed dimension sharing.
2. One Big Table (OBT): The extreme form of denormalization where ALL dimensions 
   and facts are flattened into a single, massive 100-column table. Popularized in 
   BigQuery/ClickHouse for simple BI queries, but difficult to maintain when business 
   processes expand.

3.6 CODE WALKTHROUGH: 3NF JOIN VS STAR SCHEMA JOIN

-- NORMALIZED 3NF QUERY (5 Joins - Fragile & Slow):
SELECT 
    cat.category_name,
    SUM(ol.quantity * ol.unit_price) AS total_revenue
FROM oltp.order_lines ol
JOIN oltp.orders o ON ol.order_id = o.order_id
JOIN oltp.products p ON ol.product_id = p.product_id
JOIN oltp.categories cat ON p.category_id = cat.category_id
JOIN oltp.customers c ON o.customer_id = c.customer_id
WHERE o.order_date >= '2026-01-01'
GROUP BY cat.category_name;

-- DENORMALIZED STAR SCHEMA QUERY (2 Joins - Robust & Fast):
SELECT 
    p.category_name,
    SUM(f.line_revenue) AS total_revenue
FROM olap.fact_sales f
JOIN olap.dim_product p ON f.product_key = p.product_key
JOIN olap.dim_date d ON f.order_date_key = d.date_key
WHERE d.year = 2026
GROUP BY p.category_name;

MODULE F4: NATURAL KEYS VS SURROGATE KEYS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

4.1 EXPLAIN LIKE I'M 5: NICKNAMES VS SOCIAL SECURITY NUMBERS
A Natural Key is like your nickname on the school playground: "Speedy". It makes 
sense to your friends today. But next year you break your leg, you don't run fast 
anymore, and everyone forgets that nickname! Or a new kid arrives who is also named 
"Speedy", and everyone gets confused.

A Surrogate Key is like your Government Social Security Number or Student Roll 
Number: "9482104". It doesn't describe what you look like or how fast you run, but 
it is completely unique, it never changes for your entire life, and the school 
computer never mixes you up with anyone else!


4.2 WHAT IS A NATURAL KEY AND WHY DOES IT BREAK?
A Natural Key (or Business Key) is an identifier that already exists in the real 
world or source operational system:
- Examples: Email address, Social Security Number (SSN), ISBN of a book, Product SKU, 
  Vehicle Identification Number (VIN), Customer ID issued by Shopify.

Why Natural Keys Make Catastrophic Warehouse Primary Keys:
1. Natural Keys Change: Users change email addresses. Phone numbers get recycled. 
   SKUs get modified during supply chain reorganizations. If your fact tables join 
   on email, changing an email requires updating 50 million historical fact rows!
2. Natural Keys Collide Across Systems: System A (POS) has customer '1001' (Priya). 
   System B (CRM) has customer '1001' (Rahul). Joining on natural ID creates 
   fraudulent cross-customer joins.
3. Natural Keys Leak PII: Using SSN or Email as a primary key duplicates confidential 
   Personal Identifiable Information across every index, log, and foreign key column.
4. Natural Keys are Wide and Slow: Joining on a 256-character VARCHAR email string is 
   dramatically slower than joining on an 8-byte BIGINT integer.


4.3 WHAT IS A SURROGATE KEY AND WHY WAREHOUSES INSIST ON IT?
A Surrogate Key is a system-generated, integer or UUID identifier created purely 
for the warehouse, with NO business meaning whatsoever.

Why Professional Warehouses Exclusively Use Surrogate Keys:
1. Complete Stability: A surrogate key NEVER changes. Once customer Priya is 
   assigned `customer_key = 50821`, that number never changes, even if her email, 
   name, and phone number change ten times.
2. Source System Decoupling: If your company migrates from Shopify to Salesforce, 
   and Salesforce re-indexes all customers from 1, your warehouse surrogate keys 
   protect all historical facts from breaking.
3. Enabling SCD Type 2: To maintain historical versions of an entity, the same 
   natural key (e.g., Priya `C-101`) MUST have multiple rows in the dimension table! 
   A natural key cannot be the primary key if it appears multiple times. A surrogate 
   key gives each VERSION of Priya its own unique primary key.


4.4 AUTO-INCREMENT BIGINT VS UUID V4 VS UUID V7
- Auto-Increment BIGINT:
  * 8 bytes on disk.
  * Fastest join performance; perfectly sequential; best for single-node or MPP 
    warehouses with centralized sequences.
- UUID v4 (Random):
  * 16 bytes on disk.
  * Universally unique across distributed microservices, but random ordering destroys 
    B-tree indexing and table clustering.
- UUID v7 (Time-Ordered UUID):
  * 16 bytes, embeds a 48-bit UNIX timestamp at the beginning.
  * Globally unique across distributed pipelines, while remaining sequentially 
    friendly for indexes and partition pruning.
- Warehouse Best Practice: Auto-increment / IDENTITY BIGINT is the dominant standard 
  for dimensional warehouse keys.


4.5 ENTERPRISE NAMING STANDARDS
Never name keys generically like `id`!
- `<entity>_key` : Reserved EXCLUSIVELY for Warehouse Surrogate Keys (e.g., `customer_key`, 
  `product_key`, `date_key`).
- `<entity>_id`  : Reserved EXCLUSIVELY for Source System Natural Keys (e.g., `customer_id`, 
  `order_id`, `sku`).

MODULE 4.1a: KIMBALL DIMENSIONAL MODELING - FACTS & DIMENSIONS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

5.1 EXPLAIN LIKE I'M 5: THE NUMBERS AND THE STORIES
Imagine you have a notebook.
- The Fact Table is where you write down numbers every time something happens:
  "Bought 3 chocolates for 15 dollars."
- The Dimension Tables are the storybooks that tell you all the details about those 
  numbers: Who bought them? Which store sold them? What brand was the chocolate? 
  What was the weather like that day?

Numbers without the story mean nothing ("15 dollars spent... who? where?"). 
And stories without the numbers don't tell the business if they made money. 
Putting them together makes a Star Schema!


5.2 FACT TABLES: THE NUMERIC HEARTBEAT
A Fact Table represents a business event or measurement:
- Shape: Extremely tall (millions to billions of rows), but narrow (few columns).
- Content: Composed almost exclusively of:
  1. Foreign Keys pointing to surrounding dimension tables.
  2. Degenerate dimensions (transaction identifiers like receipt numbers).
  3. Numeric measures (quantities, amounts, durations).
- Update Behavior: In traditional retail, fact tables are append-only. Once an event 
  happens, the record is written and never modified.


5.3 DIMENSION TABLES: THE RICH DESCRIPTIVE CONTEXT
A Dimension Table provides the answers to the 5 Ws: Who, What, Where, When, Why, 
and How:
- Shape: Wide (often 30 to 100+ columns), but relatively short (thousands to a few 
  million rows).
- Content: Primarily human-readable text attributes, categorizations, hierarchies, 
  and flags.
- Cardinality: Low to moderate.
- Rule: "Wider is better." In dimensions, data architects do not shy away from adding 
  rich descriptive attributes, because columnar engines only scan the columns 
  requested in a query.


5.4 HEAD-TO-HEAD COMPARISON MATRIX

+--------------------+-----------------------------+-----------------------------+
| Feature            | Fact Table                  | Dimension Table             |
+--------------------+-----------------------------+-----------------------------+
| Represents         | A business event / measure  | Descriptive context / entity|
| Key Columns        | Foreign Keys (spokes)       | Single Surrogate PK         |
| Primary Content    | Numbers (revenue, units)    | Text strings, hierarchies   |
| Row Count (Volume) | Millions to Billions (Huge) | Thousands to Millions (Small|
| Column Count(Width)| Narrow (10 - 25 columns)    | Wide (30 - 150 columns)     |
| Change Frequency   | Continuous rapid inserts    | Slowly changing over time   |
| Kimball Naming     | `fact_<business_process>`   | `dim_<subject_entity>`      |
+--------------------+-----------------------------+-----------------------------+

MODULE 4.1b: KIMBALL - THE SACRED GRAIN
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

6.1 EXPLAIN LIKE I'M 5: PHOTO RESOLUTION (4K VS THUMBNAIL)
Think of taking a photo with a high-end 4K camera. You can zoom in and see the 
individual buttons on someone's shirt. If you need a small thumbnail for an avatar, 
you can always shrink the photo.
But if you took the photo with a blurry 100-pixel webcam, you can NEVER zoom in to 
see the shirt buttons. The detail was never captured!

In data warehousing, the "Grain" is the camera resolution. If you capture sales at 
the individual item level (4K), you can easily calculate store totals, monthly totals, 
and regional totals. But if you only store store-level daily totals (thumbnail), you 
can NEVER answer: "How many bottles of apple juice did we sell?"


6.2 THE GRAIN STATEMENT: THE PIVOT OF EVERY DOWNSTREAM DECISION
Before writing a single line of SQL DDL, every data modeler must write the Grain 
Statement as a single, unambiguous English sentence:
- "One row in this fact table represents..."

Valid Grain Statements:
- "One row represents one line item on a retail sales receipt."
- "One row represents one customer account balance at month-end."
- "One row represents one patient visit milestone transition."

Invalid Grain Statements:
- "Sales data" (A topic, not a grain).
- "Orders and items" (Two conflicting grains smashed together).


6.3 THREE GRAIN LEVELS FOR THE SAME BUSINESS PROCESS
Consider retail sales. There are three possible grains:
1. Atomic Line-Item Grain:
   - Example: 1 row = 1 item scanned at the cash register.
   - Rows/year: 10,000,000.
   - Enables: Product affinity / basket analysis, product discount impact, item margins.
2. Transaction Grain:
   - Example: 1 row = 1 entire receipt (summing all items).
   - Rows/year: 2,500,000.
   - Loses: All product-level detail! Cannot tell what products were bought together.
3. Daily Summary Grain:
   - Example: 1 row = 1 store per day total sales.
   - Rows/year: 25,000.
   - Loses: Customer detail, receipt detail, item detail.


6.4 VISUAL PYRAMID: THE GRAIN RESOLUTION PYRAMID

                        / \
                       /   \       MONTHLY SNAPSHOT GRAIN
                      /     \      (500K rows/year - Coarsest)
                     /-------\     Aggregation is easy (Down -> Up)
                    /         \
                   /           \   TRANSACTION GRAIN
                  /             \  (3M rows/year - Medium)
                 /---------------\ Disaggregation is IMPOSSIBLE (Up -> Down)
                /                 \
               /                   \ LINE-ITEM GRAIN
              /                     \ (10M rows/year - Finest)
             +-----------------------+ PREFER THE FINEST ATOMIC GRAIN YOU CAN AFFORD!


6.5 THE GOLDEN RULE: PREFER THE FINEST ATOMIC GRAIN YOU CAN AFFORD
"You can always aggregate up; you can never disaggregate down."
Modern cloud warehouses (Snowflake, BigQuery, Databricks) process billions of rows 
cost-effectively. Therefore, modern data architects always capture the finest atomic 
grain produced by the operational source system. If summary tables are needed for 
speed, they are built as secondary aggregate tables (`agg_daily_sales`).


6.6 THE DEADLIEST SIN: MIXED GRAIN IN ONE FACT TABLE
Mixing grains in a single fact table is the most dangerous mistake in dimensional 
modeling:
- Imagine a table `fact_sales` where some rows represent line items (Item A = $20, 
  Item B = $30), AND other rows represent the receipt total (Order Total = $50).
- When a BI analyst runs: `SELECT SUM(revenue) FROM fact_sales;`
- The result will be $20 + $30 + $50 = $100!
- Revenue is doubled silently! No error is thrown, but executive financial dashboards 
  show false numbers.
- FIX: Never combine different grains. Build `fact_sales_line` and `fact_sales_order`.

MODULE 4.1c: CONFORMED DIMENSIONS & THE BUS ARCHITECTURE INTRO
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

7.1 WHAT IS A CONFORMED DIMENSION?
A dimension table is Conformed if it has the exact same structure, identical surrogate 
keys, and consistent business definitions across EVERY fact table in the enterprise.
- If `dim_product` has `product_key = 8841` representing a "Sony 55-inch OLED TV", 
  that same key MUST mean "Sony 55-inch OLED TV" in the Sales fact table, the 
  Inventory fact table, the Warranty fact table, and the Returns fact table!


7.2 WHY CONFORMANCE PREVENTS CORPORATE DATA CHAOS
Without conformed dimensions:
- The Sales team builds their own sales database with their own product codes.
- The Inventory team builds their own warehouse with ERP item codes.
- When the CEO asks: "Show me products where sales revenue is declining but warehouse 
  inventory is piling up," the answer is impossible because the tables cannot join.
Conformed dimensions serve as the universal data contract connecting different 
business departments.


7.3 CROSS-FACT ANALYSIS & THE DRILL-ACROSS MECHANISM
How do you combine metrics from two different fact tables?
- NEVER join Fact table directly to Fact table (A huge Cartesian fan-trap anti-pattern)!
- DRILL-ACROSS PATTERN:
  1. Aggregate Fact A (Sales) by the conformed dimension key in a Common Table 
     Expression (CTE).
  2. Aggregate Fact B (Inventory) by the conformed dimension key in a second CTE.
  3. Join the two aggregated CTEs together using the Conformed Dimension!


7.4 VISUAL GRID: KIMBALL ENTERPRISE BUS MATRIX

  BUSINESS PROCESS (Fact Table) | Date | Product | Store | Customer | Employee | Supplier
  ==============================+======+=========+=======+==========+==========+=========
  Retail Sales Transactions     |  [X] |   [X]   |  [X]  |   [X]    |   [X]    |         
  Monthly Inventory Snapshot    |  [X] |   [X]   |  [X]  |          |          |   [X]   
  Customer Returns              |  [X] |   [X]   |  [X]  |   [X]    |          |         
  Promotional Coverage          |  [X] |   [X]   |  [X]  |          |          |

  CONFORMANCE COUNT:            |  (4) |   (4)   |  (4)  |   (2)    |   (1)    |   (1)   
  * Columns with multiple [X]s are CONFORMED DIMENSIONS (built first!).
  * Drill-across queries succeed because both facts share product_key and date_key.


7.5 CODE WALKTHROUGH: CROSS-FACT DRILL-ACROSS

WITH sales_summary AS (
    SELECT 
        product_key, 
        SUM(net_revenue) AS total_sales,
        SUM(quantity_sold) AS total_units_sold
    FROM olap.fact_sales
    WHERE order_date_key BETWEEN 20260701 AND 20260731
    GROUP BY product_key
),
inventory_summary AS (
    SELECT 
        product_key,
        SUM(units_on_hand) AS ending_stock
    FROM dm_week3.fact_inventory_snapshot
    WHERE snapshot_date_key = 20260731

MODULE 4.2a, 4.2b, 4.2c: SLOWLY CHANGING DIMENSIONS (SCD)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

8.1 THE CORE DILEMMA: REAL-WORLD ENTITIES CHANGE OVER TIME
Dimension attributes change in the real world:
- Customers move to a new apartment or city.
- A product is recategorized by marketing from 'Home Decor' to 'Kitchen Appliances'.
- An employee gets promoted from 'Junior Cashier' to 'Store Manager'.
The question is: How does the warehouse store these changes without corrupting 
historical financial reports?


8.2 "AS-WAS" VS "AS-IS" REPORTING: THE PRIYA MOVE SCENARIO
- Story: Customer Priya lives in Hyderabad in Q1 (January–March) and buys ₹10,000 
  worth of groceries.
- On July 15, Priya moves to Bengaluru.
- In November, the Regional Sales Director for Hyderabad runs the Q1 performance 
  report: "What was our total revenue from Hyderabad customers in Q1?"

A. If you overwrote Priya's city to Bengaluru (SCD Type 1):
   Priya's Q1 purchases retroactively show up under Bengaluru! Hyderabad's Q1 
   revenue is cheated out of ₹10,000. This is factually false and can trigger 
   false executive decisions.

B. If you versioned Priya's record with a new row and surrogate key (SCD Type 2):
   Priya's Q1 purchases remain tied to `customer_key = 401` (Hyderabad). Her Q3 
   purchases link to `customer_key = 402` (Bengaluru). Both "As-Was" (historical) 
   and "As-Is" (current state) reports are 100% mathematically accurate!


8.3 SCD TYPE TAXONOMY AT A GLANCE

+--------+----------------------+--------------------+----------------------------------+
| Type   | Mechanism            | History Maintained | Best Production Use Case         |
+--------+----------------------+--------------------+----------------------------------+
| Type 0 | Retain Original      | Original only      | Birthdate, Original Signup Date  |
| Type 1 | Overwrite value      | None               | Typos, non-analytical attributes|
| Type 2 | Add new row (Version)| Full infinite      | City, Loyalty Tier, Salary       |
| Type 3 | Add previous column  | 1 prior state      | Territory realignment, rebrand   |
| Type 6 | Hybrid (1 + 2 + 3)   | Full + Current     | When both As-Was and As-Is needed|
+--------+----------------------+--------------------+----------------------------------+

MODULE 4.3a & 4.3b: STAR SCHEMA VS SNOWFLAKE SCHEMA
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

9.1 ANATOMY OF A STAR SCHEMA (DENORMALIZED RADIAL LAYOUT)
- Structure: A single central fact table surrounded by completely denormalized 
  dimension tables.
- Joins: Always exactly one hop (Fact <-> Dimension). Never Dimension <-> Dimension.
- Hallmarks: Query SQL is concise, joins are minimal, BI tools natively optimize 
  for it, and columnar databases compress repeated denormalized strings with ease.


9.2 ANATOMY OF A SNOWFLAKE SCHEMA (NORMALIZED SUB-DIMENSIONS)
- Structure: The dimensions of a star schema are partially or fully normalized into 
  sub-dimension hierarchy tables.
- Example: Instead of `dim_product` holding Category and Department names, it holds 
  a `category_key` pointing to `dim_category`, which holds a `department_key` 
  pointing to `dim_department`.
- Visual Shape: When drawn, the tables branch outward with multiple sub-tables, 
  resembling the crystalline structure of a snowflake.

MODULE 4.4a & 4.4b: DATA WAREHOUSE VS DATA LAKE VS LAKEHOUSE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

10.1 THE THREE LAYERS OF EVERY ANALYTICS STACK
Every data architecture consists of three distinct layers:
1. Storage Layer: Where the raw bytes live on physical hardware (SSD, S3, HDFS).
2. Metadata Layer: Defines table schemas, column data types, partitions, statistics, 
   and transaction logs.
3. Compute Layer: The query engine that reads the metadata, optimizes query plans, 
   and processes SQL operations (Snowflake Virtual Warehouses, Spark, Trino, DuckDB).


10.2 THE MEDALLION ARCHITECTURE (BRONZE, SILVER, GOLD)
The industry standard data refinement pattern in modern lakehouses:
1. Bronze (Raw Ingest Zone):
   - Append-only, untouched raw data directly from sources (e.g., raw JSON webhooks, 
     Kafka streams, CDC database dumps). Preserves complete replayability and audit trail.
2. Silver (Cleaned & Conformed Zone):
   - Data is parsed, validated, type-coerced, and deduplicated. Business logic and 
     data quality rules are enforced. Grain is standardized.
3. Gold (Business-Ready Analytical Zone):
   - High-performance Kimball Star Schemas, aggregated data marts, and feature sets 
     curated specifically for Power BI, Tableau, and ML models.

11.1 DUAL-STORE ARCHITECTURE & POINT-IN-TIME (PIT) JOINS
A Feature Store solves Train-Serve Skew by maintaining TWO storage engines 
backed by ONE single canonical feature definition:
- Offline Store (Warehouse / Lakehouse): Stores years of historical, timestamped

- Online Store (Low-Latency Key-Value Store - Redis / DynamoDB): Stores ONLY 
  the most recent feature value per entity for sub-10ms real-time inference.

16.2 H3: LATE-ARRIVING DIMENSION LIFECYCLE

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
VISUAL LIFECYCLE: LATE-ARRIVING DIMENSION HANDLING
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

  T=0: Fact arrives before Dimension
  ----------------------------------
  Fact Record: [store_code='KCH-001', revenue=₹45,000]
  1. Lookup in dim_store fails (Store not found!).
  2. Action: Create Placeholder in dim_store:
     [store_key=9042, store_code='KCH-001', store_name='PENDING', is_placeholder=1]
  3. Load Fact Record with placeholder store_key=9042. Fact is preserved!

  T=4 Hours: Real Store Record Arrives
  ------------------------------------
  Source Record: [code='KCH-001', name='Kochi MG Road Express', region='South']
  1. Execute in-place Type 1 back-update on dim_store:
     UPDATE dim_store SET store_name='Kochi MG Road Express', is_placeholder=0 WHERE store_key=9042;
  2. Fact table is UNTOUCHED! All historical queries now automatically resolve correctly!


16.3 H4: SLOWLY CHANGING FACTS & NEGATING ENTRY REVERSALS

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
VISUAL DIAGRAM: DESTRUCTIVE DELETE VS NEGATING ENTRY REVERSAL
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

  DESTRUCTIVE DELETE (Anti-Pattern):
  `DELETE FROM fact_sales WHERE date = '2026-11-05';`
  * Audit trail destroyed. Prior reports cannot be verified. Dangerous in cloud storage.

  NEGATING ENTRY REVERSAL (Production Standard):
  1. Original Duplicated Batch:   +₹1,125,000 (Load Date: Nov 05, correction_flag=0)
  2. Negating Reversal Batch:     -₹1,125,000 (Load Date: Nov 06, correction_flag=1, 'DUPLICATE')
  3. Net Sum in BI Reports:        ₹1,125,000 (Accurate net total!)
  * 100% audit trail preserved. Full history inspectable by financial auditors.
